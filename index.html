Insight #200 September 9, 2026
#GSTAT#ITC#Section73#Refunds
GSTAT Ernakulam Bench held that refund proceedings cannot be used to re-adjudicate ITC eligibility without Section 73/74 proceedings.

Key findings:

• Mandatory Route for ITC Disallowance: Any challenge to the eligibility, availment, or distribution of Input Tax Credit (ITC) must be independently determined strictly through formal proceedings under Section 73 or 74 of the CGST/SGST Act.

• Presumption of Validity: Once ITC is validly availed in statutory returns and remains untouched by Section 73/74 proceedings, it stands legally presumed to be in order. Refund applications under Section 54(3) cannot morph into collateral assessment proceedings to re-examine underlying credit eligibility.

• Binding Administrative Clarity: CBEC Circular No. 125/44/2019-GST (Paras 20 & 21) explicitly mandates that where a refund claim is sought to be rejected due to alleged ineligible ITC, the proper officer must issue a notice under Section 54 read with Section 73 or 74 to recover the wrongful credit along with interest and penalty.

• Continuity of Settled Jurisprudence: Extending historical precedents from the erstwhile CENVAT regime (Convergys India, Morgan Stanley, Qualcomm India), the Tribunal reinforced that the structural separation between credit availment, utilization, and refund remains intact under GST. Authorities cannot apply two separate yardsticks for credit utilization and refund eligibility.

#WBAAR#PureAgent#GST#ElectricityReimbursement
Hon’ble West Bengal AAR has held that recovery of electricity charges at actual cost, without any mark‑up, can qualify as pure agent reimbursement and remain outside GST.

Two significant observations stand out:

• Rule 33 vs Circular: Even after finding that the applicant fails the pure‑agent test under Rule 33 of the CGST Rules, the AAR held that the deeming fiction in the second sentence of Para 3.3 still applies – where electricity is recovered from occupants at the same amount charged by the DISCOM and without any profit element, the supplier is deemed to act as a pure agent, and GST does not apply.

• Scope widened: The AAR noted that Para 3.3 of Circular No. 206/18/2023‑GST begins with the word “however”, creating an exception to the general principle that electricity supplied along with maintenance/renting forms part of a taxable composite supply. Importantly, the expression “etc.” after Real Estate Owners, RWAs and Developers widens the scope of the Circular to other entities as well, so long as the nature of supply is similar.

Impact: The Circular’s deeming provision operates independently of strict Rule 33 conditions. This reasoning may support actual‑cost electricity recovery by facility managers, maintenance companies and similar arrangements, beyond traditional real estate setups.

Subsequent sale or remanufacturing of imported goods determines customs value
Recently the Hon’ble US CBP has addressed valuation of used, damaged or defective transaxle “cores” returned to the US under a core-exchange programme...
Insight #195 September 5, 2026
#Customs#Valuation#USCBP
Recently the Hon’ble US CBP has addressed valuation of used, damaged or defective transaxle “cores” returned to the US under a core-exchange programme.

• Toyota Canada returned used cores to its US remanufacturer. The cores were not sold to the US at the time of import; the payment received was only a refund of the earlier $1,000 core deposit.

• CBP therefore held that the transaction value method was not available.

• Instead, CBP permitted use of the fallback method, with the eventual treatment of the imported core becoming relevant:

    – If the core is subsequently sold as-is → value can be based on the sale price of comparable cores.

    – If the core is remanufactured → value can be based on the subsequent sale price of the remanufactured product, less the average cost of repair/remanufacturing.

• Since the importer may not know at the time of import which route the core will take, CBP recommended Reconciliation to determine the final value subsequently.

📌 The interesting takeaway: Even though there is no sale at the time of import, subsequent commercial transactions can provide the basis for determining customs value under the fallback method.

#SupremeCourt#Rule96#IGSTRefund
Hon'ble SC has held that the omission of Rule 96(10) of the CGST Rules with effect from 8 October 2024 applies even to pending refund proceedings.

The Court relied on the Constitution Bench ruling in Kolhapur Canesugar Works and held that when a rule is omitted, pending proceedings under that rule can continue only if there is a saving clause or other legal mechanism preserving them.

Since Rule 96(10) was omitted without any saving clause, the restriction cannot continue to apply merely because the refund proceedings were pending when the rule was omitted.

Interestingly, the Court also noted that the GST Council's recommendation for prospective omission was only advisory and could not override the legal effect of the omission.

Impact: Pending IGST refund disputes involving Rule 96(10) will now have to be considered without applying the omitted restriction.

The Supreme Court has also directed High Courts to take up pending matters on this issue expeditiously to bring the litigation to a close.

GSTAT's First Ruling on 'Intermediary' Services — Dow India
The GSTAT's first ruling on what constitutes an "intermediary" under Section 2(13) of the IGST Act...
Insight #90
#GSTAT#Intermediary#PlaceOfSupply
The GSTAT's first ruling on the vexed question of what constitutes an "intermediary" under Section 2(13) of the IGST Act.

The facts: Dow India had paid IGST under RCM treating procurement facilitation services received from Dow Europe GmbH (its Swiss group entity, functioning as the centralised procurement hub for the entire global Dow Group) as an import of services. It subsequently changed its position, arguing that Dow Europe was an "intermediary" — which, under Section 13(8)(b) IGST Act (since omitted by Finance Act 2026 w.e.f. 30.03.2026), would have fixed the place of supply at the supplier's location in Switzerland, outside India, making the transaction non-taxable.

GSTAT applied the three-limb framework from CBIC Circular 159/15/2021 and held:

— while the first condition of minimum three parties (Dow Europe, Dow India, and the third-party foreign suppliers) was satisfied,
— the second condition i.e. two distinct supplies, namely a main supply between two principals and a separate ancillary facilitation supply was not.

The critical reasoning: Dow Europe's procurement hub function is not a subsidiary or facilitative activity rendered for Dow India's benefit; it is Dow Europe's core business, performed for the entire Dow Group globally.

Placing reliance on the Karnataka HC ruling in Columbia Sportswear and the Delhi HC ruling in Blackberry India, GSTAT held that Dow Europe rendered services on its own account as an independent contractor with no authority to bind Dow India, satisfying the exclusionary limb of Section 2(13) — and the transaction remained an import of services taxable in India.

Two important ancillary holdings:

First, GSTAT flagged Revenue's contradictory posture — treating outbound group services by Indian entities as intermediary (to deny export/zero-rating) while simultaneously treating inbound group services from foreign entities as import of services (to levy GST) and observed that both positions cannot simultaneously hold, one consistent view must apply; and

Second, GSTAT correctly disagreed with the First Appellate Authority's "afterthought" reasoning, holding that a refund claim filed under Section 54 CGST Act cannot be dismissed merely because the taxpayer initially paid tax under a mistaken legal understanding — the statutory refund mechanism exists precisely for such situations and must be examined on merits.

The importer determined the value of goods imported from its overseas group entities...
Insight #120
#CESTAT#TransferPricing#CustomsValuation
Brief Background: The importer determined the value of goods imported from its overseas group entities under a global ROVAC (Return on Value Added Cost) transfer pricing policy, designed to ensure that the Indian distributor earned a predetermined return. Customs disputed the declared import values, leading the Tribunal to undertake an extensive examination of the interplay between transfer pricing and customs valuation.

Key Tribunal Observations:

📌 Transfer Pricing (TP) Rules ≠ Customs Valuation Rules
The Tribunal held that OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines and income-tax principles serve a different purpose from Customs valuation. While transfer pricing examines profitability of associated enterprises over a representative period, Customs valuation is transaction-specific and governed exclusively by Section 14 of the Customs Act and the Customs Valuation Rules. A pricing methodology designed to ensure the Indian distributor earns a predetermined return does not automatically establish that the declared import price satisfies the Customs Valuation Rules.

📌 Higher Income-Tax Payment Does Not Cure Customs Valuation
Higher profits and consequent higher income-tax payment in India do not establish that the declared import value satisfies the Customs Valuation Rules.

📌 Deductive Valuation Cannot Result in Double Additions
Having adopted the deductive value method, Customs cannot again add international freight and insurance where those elements are already embedded in the resale price.

📌 Discounts Must Independently Satisfy Customs Valuation Rules
Each category of discount — special negotiated discounts, price protection, end-of-life discounts, cooperative marketing discounts and influencer payments — was examined separately. Admissibility depends on the Customs Valuation Rules and evidence, rather than treatment under the transfer pricing policy.

In a significant ruling, Hon'ble Uttarakhand AAAR has held that GST exemption for healthcare services cannot be denied merely because the services are provided under a contract with a Government implementing agency.

The case involved a private operator engaged to run Government Urban Health & Wellness Centres and Polyclinics under the Ayushman Bharat scheme, where doctors, nurses and other medical staff were deployed to provide free healthcare to the public.

The tax authorities had treated the arrangement as a taxable operation and management service since the contract and payments were routed through a Government PSU.

Rejecting this approach, the AAAR held that the true test is the substance of the supply and not the contractual payment mechanism. It observed that the PSU was only an implementing agency, while the dominant activity undertaken by the operator was the provision of healthcare through clinical establishments. Accordingly, the Authority ruled that the services retained their character as exempt healthcare services under Entry 74 of Notification No. 12/2017-CT (Rate), emphasizing that the identity of the payer cannot alter the intrinsic nature of the supply.

SEZ Units Can Now Sell into India at Concessional Duty — But the Fine Print Needs Attention
Central Government opened concessional customs duty window for SEZ units selling into DTA...
Insight #181
#SEZ#DTASales#CustomsNotification
In a significant development, the Central Government has opened a concessional customs duty window for SEZ units selling manufactured goods into the Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) vide Notification No. 11/2026-Customs dated 31st March 2026, effective 1st April 2026 through 31st March 2027.

BCD is capped at 4% to 15% across a wide range of products — chemicals, textiles, machinery, electronics, medical devices and consumer goods. For select items like footwear, solar cells, PVC flex films and furniture, AIDC is additionally capped.

Who qualifies:
• Units that started production on or before 31st March 2025
• Goods must be genuinely manufactured — repacking, relabeling or repair does not count
• Minimum 20% value addition required
• DTA sales under this window capped at 30% of the unit's highest annual FOB export value in any of the preceding three years

Who is out:
• Units in Free Trade and Warehousing Zones
• Goods merely imported into the SEZ and re-cleared to DTA as-is
• Units that commenced production after 31st March 2025

A point of caution:

Audit risk: SEZ units availing this benefit are subject to audit under Rule 79 of the SEZ Rules. This is not a procedural formality but conditions are detailed and a breach means full duty recovery.

The export benefit condition: The benefit is lost if any DTA input supplier has claimed duty drawback or FTP export benefits on inputs supplied to the SEZ unit. The unit has no legal control over its suppliers' decisions, yet bears the consequence. Worse, the notification does not define "export benefit", courts have in some contexts treated even the IGST refund route as an export benefit, leaving the question open.

IGST is separate: This notification only caps BCD and AIDC. IGST on SEZ-to-DTA sales continues to be governed under GST law as an inter-state supply — the DTA buyer pays and claims ITC normally.

Orissa HC quashed Order-in-Original under Section 74 where authority relied on DGGI Alert...
Insight #189
#Section74#FakeInvoice#ITCReversal
Hon'ble HC has quashed an Order-in-Original under Section 74 where the adjudicating authority had mechanically relied on a DGGI Alert Notice (supplier found non-existent) to demand tax, interest and penalty from the recipient — completely ignoring that the recipient had voluntarily reversed the entire ITC even before the SCN was issued.

The Court held:
(i) a third-party supplier's admission of issuing fake invoices does not ipso facto establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression on the recipient's part; the adjudicating authority must independently examine the recipient's complicity and cannot blindly adopt the DGGI alert;

(ii) the voluntary pre-SCN reversal of ITC is itself evidence of absence of intent to evade;

(iii) since the Electronic Credit Ledger carried a surplus balance throughout, no interest is leviable under Sec. 50(3) r/w Rule 88B and Circular 192/2023;

(iv) wrongly availed ITC is "utilised" only when the ECL balance falls below the impugned amount; and

(v) demanding tax equivalent to ITC already reversed and then imposing an equal penalty amounts to double taxation, making the penalty order wholly unsustainable.

The Court also flagged that Sec. 74 was consciously invoked only after the Sec. 73 limitation period had lapsed, reinforcing that Sec. 74 cannot be a fallback for time-barred demands dressed up as fraud cases.
